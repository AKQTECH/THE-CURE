The Cure

A guide for the writer who knows what you want to write and can't start. Not because you don't know how. Because the knowing feels insufficient as a reason to begin.

What's Actually Happening

The Mechanism

This is not a craft problem. It is not writer's block in any useful sense. The mechanism is specific, and you have already named it: you have collapsed the distance between the writing and the writer's identity. A bad draft doesn't mean "this needs revision." It means you are not who you think you are.

The Gap

The scene stays vivid in your head indefinitely because the internal version never has to be tested. The moment you start typing, it enters the zone where it can fail. And failure doesn't mean a bad scene. It means the discovery that the gap between what you can imagine and what you can execute is permanent.

That's the fear. Not a bad script. A permanent demotion from the version in your head.

The Adjacent Task

The research, the notes, the reformatting, the app-building. You built an entire screenwriting tool to avoid using it. That's not procrastination. That's your protector part being incredibly resourceful. She maintains the fiction that you're on your way. The internal version stays intact, the identity stays intact, and nothing has been disproved yet.

The Success Trap

The writing was better than expected, and that made it worse. Now the bar is set by an accident. Even success becomes a threat because the accidental good thing raises the standard for the next intentional attempt. The brief high, then the auditor arrives to evaluate. There is no winning inside that system.

The Sneak Attack

The voice doesn't always stop you from opening the document. Sometimes it waits. You get going, twenty minutes in, and then: "Is that part good enough?" "Is that cheesy?" You engage with it. Twenty minutes pass before you notice you stopped writing and started arguing. That's the version that's hardest to catch, because you were already in.

It's not that you need the outcome to be perfect. It's that you want to matter.

From Your Therapist, April 2026

The New Approach

You've been fighting the voice. Every strategy so far has been about outmaneuvering her, circumventing her, tricking her into silence. Your therapist pointed out something you'd never tried: what if you just heard her out?

Don't Fight. Listen.

Resistance gives the voice power. The more you push against it, the louder it gets. Instead: pause. Let her talk. What is she actually saying? When you write it down — when you journal what the voice is telling you — it doesn't hold up. It falls apart on the page. The voice is confident in the dark. It is not confident in writing.

Feel It in Your Body

The voice has a location. Chest, throat, jaw, hands. Before you try to think your way through it, find where she lives in your body right now. Breathe into that spot. Hold it there. Exhale slowly. You're not solving anything — you're just making room. The somatic approach works because the voice operates below thought. You can't outthink something that isn't thinking.

The ADHD Factor

Taking a break from writing doesn't mean a 10-minute pause. It means you're oiling teak furniture at 2 AM and the whole night is gone. The task-switching cost is not normal-sized for you. Which means the "just take a break and come back to it" advice is wrong. The session rules below are built around this: bounded time, forward only, no breaks that let the night escape.

Journal the Voice

When the auditor shows up mid-session, don't argue with her. Open a separate note (not the draft) and write down exactly what she's saying. Word for word. "This scene is cheesy." "You're not ready." "This isn't what Lilly expects." When you see it written out, it loses its authority. The voice is a feeling pretending to be a fact. Facts survive paper. Feelings pretending to be facts do not.

"I've literally never tried hearing the voice out before."

Five Minutes Max

The Pre-Writing Ritual

The ritual isn't preparation. It's permission delivered through muscle memory. It is specifically designed to be too short to become an adjacent task.

The Checklist

Say it out loud: "I am a writer who writes." Present tense. Declarative. No qualification. You're telling the protector who you already are.
Play the song. Same song every time. It's Pavlovian — you're training your nervous system to associate it with the unguarded room.
Get the glass of water. Sit in the spot. No reviewing notes, no reading the previous scene. The ritual has to be too short to think in.
Open a new document. Not where you stopped. New. Blank. Opening previous work puts you in comparison mode. New document removes that. You're not continuing — you're discovering.
Write one bridge sentence. Not the first line of the scene. Just a door — "she was already tired of explaining it" or "the room felt different in the afternoon." It doesn't have to be good. It's a door, not a sentence.

Match Your Energy

Writing Modes

Check in with what your brain can actually do right now — not what the calendar says. Each mode is a real session. Each one counts.

Flow Mode

High activation, ideas firing. New scenes, dialogue, discovery writing.

Note-to-Scene

Medium energy. Take an existing note and expand it into a scene.

Cut Mode

Surgical, detached. Edit existing work. Remove, tighten, restructure.

Friction Mode

Auditor is loud. Breathe. Journal the voice. Bridge sentence only.

Flow Mode Rules

New document. Bridge sentence. Forward only. Don't read back. Don't stop until the tank starts emptying — but stop before it's empty. Leave a door line at the bottom for next time. This is the 10-hour, forget-to-eat mode. Don't fight it, don't schedule it, just protect it when it arrives.

Note-to-Scene Rules

Pick one note. Just one. Open a new document, paste the note at the top, then write the scene below it. The note is the seed — not the outline, not the constraint. Let the scene go wherever it goes. If it departs from the note, the scene is right and the note was the seed's job, now finished.

Cut Mode Rules

Only available when you have three or more scenes. You're not generating — you're shaping. The auditor is useful here. Let her look. This is where she belongs: after the draft exists, not before. Give her the scalpel but not the pen.

Friction Mode Rules

The auditor is loud and the document feels impossible. Don't fight. Use the breathing exercise above. Open a separate note and journal exactly what the voice is saying. Read it back. It won't hold up. Then: one bridge sentence in a new document. That's the whole session. If more comes, let it. If it doesn't, the bridge sentence counts. Twenty minutes counts. You showed up.

For the Session

The Rules

1

Forward Only

When a sentence feels wrong, type XXX and keep going. The XXX marks are a promise: I will come back for you. They keep the generative brain in charge. You are not allowed to go back during a session.

2

One Thing Per Sitting

Not one thing per day. One thing per sitting. When you feel the tank emptying, stop. Check what mode your brain is actually in. The session is over when it's over, not when the clock says.

3

Stop Before Empty

Stop while you still know what comes next. Write it as a single line at the bottom — just enough: "she asks him about the flight" or "this is where he lies." That line is the door you leave open. Next session starts from that line.

4

Don't Read It Back

Not today. Not tomorrow. You can read it when you have three scenes, minimum. One scene is too easy for the auditor to dismiss. Three scenes is a thing. Three scenes has weight she has to reckon with.

5

Close the Loop Outside the Draft

After the session ends, before anything else: send one voice note. To Sameliah, to a trusted reader, to yourself. Something specific about the aliveness of having done it. The voice note closes the validation loop the auditor was providing. The draft stays closed.

6

No Breaks That Cost the Night

ADHD means a break isn't a break — it's a portal to oiling teak furniture at 2 AM. If you need to step away, set a timer for the pause. Five minutes. Phone facedown. Then back. The night is not renewable.

Things That Are True

Forensic, Not Aspirational

The only condition under which you write freely is when you aren't trying — when the auditor doesn't have time to arrive.

That's the proof of concept.

You are not trying to become a writer. You already are one.

The part of you calling one scene insufficient is the same part that will call ten scenes insufficient.

There is no amount that satisfies her. So the scene you wrote counts.

Your protector part will build entire software products to stay adjacent to the writing.

That is not failure. That is evidence of how badly you want this.

The understanding has become its own adjacent task.

The gap isn't insight. It's the ten seconds between knowing and starting.

When the voice is written down — journaled, word for word — it doesn't hold up.

The voice is confident in the dark. It falls apart on paper.

The short story that became Pick Me came from the room where no one was watching.

That room still exists. You just have to enter it without the audience already seated.

Permission to produce imperfect work and call it real — that's what you said you need.

The Minimum Viable Session

You don't need an hour. You don't need a scene. You need one bridge sentence in a new document. If more comes, it comes. If it doesn't, the bridge sentence counts. Twenty minutes counts. One scene counts. The only rule is forward only, tween April in the room, auditor gets nothing until there are three.

The Free Water

Remember what your therapist helped you see: the image of water pouring, the feeling of freedom to write. That image is real. It's yours. It's why the glass of water is at the top of this page. The water is free. The writing is free. You don't have to earn the right to pour it out. You just pour.

Granting permission is not lowering the bar. It's refusing to let the bar be the point.

If You Need to Hear It Right Now

The scene you wrote is real. It's real. Write the next one with the same low stakes you somehow found for that one — and don't look back at it. Forward only. The auditor lives in the rearview.

You are a writer who writes. Not because the draft proves it. Because the knowing was always enough.

You kept me safe. Every time I didn't write and someone couldn't read something unfinished and find it lacking, you did that. Every time the internal version stayed perfect and untouchable, you protected it. You have been doing your job.

The ask isn't "let me write without you." The ask is: can you protect me differently? Instead of protecting me from the attempt, can you protect me during it? Can you be the part that reminds me the draft doesn't define me — rather than the part that prevents the draft?

And when you show up mid-sentence with "is that cheesy?" — I'm going to try something new. I'm going to hear you out. I'm going to write down exactly what you say. And then I'm going to keep writing. Because I've literally never tried that before. And you deserve to be heard. You just don't get to be the editor. Not yet.
